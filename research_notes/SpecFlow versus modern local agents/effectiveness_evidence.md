# Effectiveness evidence: SpecFlow versus modern local coding agents

Research checked October 28, 2026. Scope: developer-operated agents working in local repositories, including terminal/IDE agents using remote model APIs. Hosted issue-to-code services and hosted PR review are not recommendations or comparison targets here. Benchmark issue-repair experiments are included only as controlled component evidence, not as measurements of local developer productivity. Evidence labels below distinguish experiments, observational research, and vendor engineering experience. Recommendations and inferences are explicitly labeled.

## 1. What do controlled studies and field research establish about productivity?

### Takeaway

There is credible evidence both for gains and for overhead, in different populations and tool generations. None of the verified sources directly compares SpecFlow with a well-configured native local agent. The defensible claim is that a staged framework is a testable intervention, not an established productivity improvement.

### Cited Findings

**METR: the unusually relevant local-work randomized experiment.**

- **Independent RCT; published July 10, 2025; work February–June 2025.** Sixteen experienced open-source contributors supplied 246 real tasks in repositories they knew well. Issues were randomized to AI allowed or disallowed before implementation. Repositories averaged over 22,000 stars and one million lines; tasks averaged about two hours. AI use was mostly Cursor Pro with Claude 3.5/3.7 Sonnet. Developers recorded screens and self-reported implementation time; compensation was $150/hour. AI-allowed work took **19% longer**, with the later METR summary giving a confidence interval of **+2% to +39%**. Developers expected a 24% speedup and afterward perceived a 20% speedup. Supporting extract: “when developers use AI tools, they take 19% longer than without.” [METR original study](https://metr.org/blog/2025-07-10-early-2025-ai-experienced-os-dev-study/); [paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.09089); [CI and update](https://metr.org/blog/2026-02-24-uplift-update/).
- **Interpretation limits.** This is stronger evidence about experienced maintainers doing familiar, high-standard repository work than about beginners, greenfield projects, or late-2026 agents. METR explicitly did not establish that no better prompting/scaffolding could have helped. It reports similar submitted quality across conditions, but the task standard includes implicit repository expectations beyond passing a fixed test suite. The current original-study page explicitly says its historical results no longer reflect current model impact. Supporting extract: “We view this result as a snapshot of early-2025 AI capabilities in one relevant setting.” [Original study, methodology, caveats, and current banner](https://metr.org/blog/2025-07-10-early-2025-ai-experienced-os-dev-study/).

**METR follow-up: important change, not a clean reversal.**

- **Published February 24, 2026; follow-up began August 2025.** The later pool comprised **57 developers, 143 repositories, and 800+ tasks**: ten original participants and 47 new recruits. Median programming experience was ten years; pay fell to $50/hour. Returning developers' estimated time effect was **−18%**, CI **−38% to +9%**; new developers' estimate was **−4%**, CI **−15% to +9%**. Both intervals include no effect. Supporting extract: “our data is only very weak evidence for the size of this increase.” [METR follow-up](https://metr.org/blog/2026-02-24-uplift-update/).
- **Threats are substantive.** Developers increasingly declined participation rather than work without AI; 30–50% of surveyed participants reported withholding some tasks for this reason. Some failed to complete AI-disallowed assignments. Quality and task choice could change under AI. Concurrent agents made task-time reports unreliable because developers did unrelated work during waits. Lower compensation is another recruitment confound. METR suspects the measured uplift is biased downward, but that directional interpretation is not a corrected causal estimate. Supporting extract: “the data from our new experiment gives us an unreliable signal of the current productivity effect of AI tools.” [Follow-up, selection effects and study-design changes](https://metr.org/blog/2026-02-24-uplift-update/).

**METR's latest verified productivity publication in this search is a survey, not another definitive RCT.**

- **Published May 11, 2026; survey February–April 2026.** Clean sample **349 technical workers**, including **87 software engineers**; 359 raw respondents with ten filtered for answer-quality problems. Median self-reported value increase was **1.4–2×**, depending on question wording; median reported speed increase was **3×**. Half regularly used Claude Code. This was a convenience sample, with approximately 2% response to emailed invitations, and included researchers, academics, founders, and managers. Supporting extract: “survey results are not necessarily grounded in reality.” It is evidence of perceived utility/adoption and a warning to distinguish speed from value, not a measured 2× developer productivity gain. [METR survey](https://metr.org/blog/2026-05-11-ai-usage-survey/).

**Large positive field experiments exist, but do not test staged agent workflows.**

- **Company-run randomized field experiments; Microsoft research record dated June 2025.** Cui et al. pooled **4,867 developers** at Microsoft, Accenture, and an anonymous Fortune 100 firm. Random subsets received an AI assistant providing intelligent code completions. The reported pooled increase in completed tasks among tool users was **26.08%**, **SE 10.3%**; less-experienced developers had higher adoption and larger gains. Supporting extract: “Though each experiment is noisy, when data is combined across three experiments and 4,867 developers…” This is not a Claude Code/Codex agent-loop or SpecFlow trial. Assignment to access, actual adoption, and the user-effect estimator should not be conflated. The landing page does not establish long-run maintainability or equivalent task complexity. Authors include Microsoft researchers; this is research evidence, not an independent product comparison. [Primary research record and preprint link](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/publication/the-effects-of-generative-ai-on-high-skilled-work-evidence-from-three-field-experiments-with-software-developers/).

**Newer large observational evidence separates writing from shipping.**

- **NBER working paper 35275; issued May 2026, revised September 2026.** Demirer, Musolff, and Yang combine AI telemetry with data on **over 500,000 GitHub developers**, using a **matched event-study design**, not random assignment. The current abstract reports cumulative commit-activity effects of **30% for autocomplete, 180% for interactive agents, and 240% for autonomous agents**. Across the full production hierarchy, the 240% effect attenuates to **80% for project count and 30% for releases**; across four software marketplaces, new app counts rise without increased total usage. These are cumulative estimates across tool generations, not isolated marginal gains, and not speed measurements. Supporting extract: “Large task-level AI productivity gains have therefore translated only partially into shipped and used software thus far.” Two authors disclose paid Microsoft consulting. [Primary paper record, current abstract, dates, and disclosures](https://www.nber.org/papers/w35275).
- **Scope caution on that paper.** Its aggregate results mix interactive and autonomous tool generations. The autonomous/hosted portion is outside the requested comparison; no hosted-service ROI is inferred here. The relevant lessons are that observational estimates need selection assumptions and that commits are not shipped value. The abstract alone is insufficient to assign the large figures to a particular local agent setup or infer local human time saved. [NBER primary record](https://www.nber.org/papers/w35275).

**Field research makes human steering visible.**

- **Exploratory interview research; preprint June 3, 2026; interviews July–August 2025.** Dhanorkar, Passi, and Vorvoreanu interviewed **17 experienced agent users** for roughly an hour each; twelve worked at the authors' organization, Microsoft. Sixteen used agents several times weekly; twelve daily. They identify four kinds of oversight: **a priori controls, co-planning, real-time monitoring, and post-hoc review**. Participants used shortcuts such as assuming a plan reflected actual execution or treating green tests as guarantees. Supporting extract: “developers opt for efficient, not perfect, oversight.” This establishes practices and difficulties, not the causal productivity effect of planning or the prevalence of failure across all developers. Sampling was snowball/intensity-based and concentrated in well-resourced organizations. [Full primary paper, methods and findings](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.05391v1).

### Inferences

- **Inference:** “AI helps” and “our framework adds value beyond native AI” are separate hypotheses. None of the AI-versus-no-AI effects identifies the incremental value of SpecFlow.
- **Inference:** METR's perception gap makes remembered productivity and agent enthusiasm unsuitable as primary outcomes. Record time and accepted outcomes prospectively.
- **Inference:** Agent waiting time, human active time, review time, and shipped value need separate accounting. A more expensive, slower agent run can still save attention; more commits can fail to improve the product. This follows from METR's concurrency problems and the writing-versus-shipping distinction.
- **Inference:** A framework may help externalize tacit requirements or coordinate a team even without speeding a single task. Those are distinct outcomes requiring their own measures.

### Gaps

- No verified randomized local-repo comparison of SpecFlow, another mandatory full staged specification pipeline, and an equally capable native agent was found.
- No reliable late-2026 numeric local speedup can be inferred from the early METR RCT, its selection-affected follow-up, or the May survey. Latest verified METR publication relevant here: **May 11, 2026**; latest experimental-design update: **February 24, 2026**. This is the latest found in this bounded search, not a guarantee that no other publication exists.
- Sarkar's *AI Agents and Higher-Order Work* appeared in search and is cited by METR and other research, but its SSRN primary page returned HTTP 403. Its reported effect sizes are intentionally not used as independently verified evidence here. [Unretrieved primary record](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=5713646).

## 2. Do instructions, specifications, context management, tests, and multiple agents improve outcomes?

### Takeaway

Useful structure can improve reliability, but more detail, more stages, and more agents are not monotonically better. The strongest component evidence supports testing the actual intervention; vendor experience specifically recommends removing scaffolding when a new model makes it redundant.

### Cited Findings

**Repository instruction files: use the September revision, not February headlines.**

- **Controlled benchmark ablation; Gloaguen et al.; first submitted February 12, 2026, latest verified revision September 29, 2026, v3.** The benchmark is called **CTXbench** in v3, versus AGENTbench in v1. It has **138 tasks in twelve Python repositories** with developer-committed instructions, plus **300 SWE-bench Lite tasks in eleven repositories** for generated-file comparisons. Four model/agent configurations: Claude Code/Sonnet 4.5; Codex/GPT-5.2; Codex/GPT-5.1 mini; Qwen Code/Qwen3-30b-coder. One trajectory per instance/setting. It compares no instruction file, agent-generated instructions, and developer-committed instructions. This is autonomous benchmark task resolution, not developer productivity. [Current full paper](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.11988v3); [version history](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.11988).
- **Current statistical result:** generated files reduce average resolution rates by the authors' reported **0.5% on SWE-bench Lite and 2% on CTXbench**, but neither difference is statistically significant (**p=.87 and .37**). Developer-committed files improve by reported **2.4% on average**, also nonsignificant (**p=.21**), while significantly outperforming generated files (**p=.038**). The headline comparison of developer versus generated files is described as 7% in the introduction. Do not silently convert these differently described percentages into percentage-point effects. Supporting extract: “LLM-generated context files have a marginal negative effect on task success rates, while developer-written ones provide a marginal performance gain, neither statistically significant.” [v3, sections 4.2 and 6, Table 3](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.11988v3).
- **Cost and behavior are clearer than success effects.** Generated instructions increase average inference cost **20% and 23%** on the two datasets; agents take more steps and perform more tests/exploration. Developer files also increase steps and cost. Agents generally follow the instructions, including repository-specific tool requests; failure to improve is not simply failure to read them. Overviews do not reduce time-to-first-relevant-file. Supporting extract: “while instructions in the context files are well followed by coding agents, repository overviews…are not helpful.” [v3 abstract and sections 4.2–4.3](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.11988v3).
- **Important qualifications:** Python only; test-based correctness, not long-term security, performance, style, or organizational consistency. CTXbench descriptions and tests were partly generated with access to reference patches, then reviewed; tests averaged 75% coverage of changed code. This favors well-specified testable work and does not measure eliciting ambiguous stakeholder requirements. Developer-committed does not guarantee entirely human-authored. Length/category ablations found no significant influence, so this paper does **not** establish a universal optimal line count or that shorter always wins. Removing other documentation makes generated context more useful, consistent with redundancy being part of the problem. Supporting extract: “the length of context files has no significant impact on our results.” [v3 methods, sections 4.4–5, appendices B–C](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.11988v3).

**Workflow plans: some structure helps; extra phases can hurt.**

- **Benchmark experiment; preprint April 13, 2026.** *From Plan to Action* analyzes **16,991 SWE-agent trajectories**, four models (**GPT-5 mini, DeepSeek-V3, DeepSeek-R1, Devstral-small**), eight plan variants, SWE-bench Verified and Python SWE-bench Pro. Primary setup uses all 500 Verified tasks. The standard plan is navigate → reproduce → patch → validate. Removing the standard plan reduced overall resolution across these models; periodically reminding the agent could improve results. Adding task-relevant early regression-testing phases could instead hurt. Supporting extracts: “A subpar plan hurts performance even more than no plan at all” and “augmenting a plan with additional task-relevant phases in the early stage can degrade performance.” [Full primary paper](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.12147v1).
- **Limits of that finding:** “plan” here is a workflow instruction, not a long product/architecture specification. The experiment does not compare modern native agents with their plans intact against a custom framework. Plan compliance is not a reliable substitute for outcome quality: GPT-5 mini had a weak, nonsignificant negative compliance/success association in one analysis; model-specific internalized strategies differ. Explanations involving contamination or internal model strategy are hypotheses, not directly observed causal mechanisms. [Sections 3–6 and threats to validity](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.12147v1).

**Context engineering recommendations: vendor guidance, not a local RCT.**

- **Anthropic engineering, September 29, 2025.** Recommends small sets of high-signal context, just-in-time file retrieval, compaction, persistent notes, and isolated subagent contexts where appropriate. Start with a minimal prompt and add guidance in response to observed failures. Supporting extracts: “minimal does not necessarily mean short” and “smarter models require less prescriptive engineering.” This is informed engineering advice; it does not quantify a SpecFlow benefit. Native Claude Code already uses instructions plus glob/grep retrieval and compaction, so a comparison cannot assume the native alternative has no context management. [Primary engineering post](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/effective-context-engineering-for-ai-agents).
- **OpenAI engineering case study, February 11, 2026.** Reports five months building an internal product, initially three engineers, about 1,500 merged changes, and roughly one million lines. The estimated 1/10th manual development time has no randomized or measured manual counterfactual. Relevant local lessons: roughly 100-line `AGENTS.md` as a map; indexed repository documents; lightweight plans for small changes, durable plans for complex ones; executable architectural checks; worktree-local app/browser/observability tools. Supporting extracts: “give Codex a map, not a 1,000-page instruction manual” and “This behavior depends heavily on the specific structure and tooling of this repository and should not be assumed to generalize.” Hosted review/merge practices in that post are outside this assignment and are not recommended here. [Primary case study](https://openai.com/index/harness-engineering/).

**Autonomous loops and tests: useful mechanisms, incomplete comparative evidence.**

- **Anthropic engineering, November 26, 2025.** Reports that looping an agent with compaction and a vague app-building prompt still produced half-finished work and premature completion. Their initializer creates a feature list, startup script, progress file, and git checkpoint; later sessions implement one feature and leave handoff state. Browser-based verification exposed problems unit tests or curl missed. Supporting extract: “compaction isn’t sufficient.” No sample size, controlled effect estimate, or budget-matched comparison is published. It explicitly leaves single versus multi-agent superiority unresolved. [Primary post](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/effective-harnesses-for-long-running-agents).
- **Anthropic engineering, March 24, 2026: particularly relevant capability-dependent evidence.** A planner/generator/evaluator setup produced better demonstrated full-stack applications than a solo baseline, but one example cost **$200 and six hours versus $9 and twenty minutes**. Output scope also grew substantially. This is a case comparison, not an equal-budget local productivity trial. After moving to Opus 4.6, the author removed sprint decomposition; after earlier model improvements, forced context resets could be dropped. Evaluator passes became overhead on tasks the stronger generator handled reliably, yet still caught missing functionality on difficult tasks. Supporting extract: “every component in a harness encodes an assumption about what the model can't do on its own…those assumptions…can quickly go stale as models improve.” [Primary post](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/harness-design-long-running-apps).
- **Specification-detail lesson from the same source:** the planner was instructed to focus on product intent and high-level design, not granular implementation, because a mistaken early technical prescription could propagate. Planner-generated scope expansion was considered a benefit by the author, but can be wasted work when the actual user wants a narrowly scoped change. The post demonstrates that evaluation criteria steer output and that QA agents require calibration; separating agents alone did not make the reviewer reliable. [Planner design and evaluator tuning](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/harness-design-long-running-apps).
- **Anthropic compiler capability demonstration, February 5, 2026.** Sixteen parallel agents, nearly 2,000 sessions over two weeks, about **$20,000**, produced a 100,000-line C compiler. The researcher spent substantial effort on verifiers, readable test output, deterministic fast test subsets, progress artifacts, and decomposable tasks. Sixteen agents were ineffective when all attacked the same kernel-compilation bottleneck; changing the verifier enabled independent work. The result had important limitations: poorer code generation than GCC, incomplete toolchain pieces, code-quality limits, and regressions. Supporting extracts: “the task verifier is nearly perfect, otherwise Claude will solve the wrong problem” and “Having 16 agents running didn't help because each was stuck solving the same task.” A one-project capability demonstration does not establish sixteen agents beat one at equal cost. [Primary experiment report](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/building-c-compiler).

**Multiple agents: preserve task-domain and budget boundaries.**

- **Google Research controlled systems evaluation, summarized January 28, 2026.** **180 configurations**, five architectures, three model families, and four benchmarks (Finance-Agent, BrowseComp-Plus, PlanCraft, Workbench). Multi-agent coordination helped parallelizable work but hurt sequential tasks; the report gives **39–70% degradation** for multi-agent variants on PlanCraft. These are not coding-repo productivity tasks, so do not import those percentages into a SpecFlow ROI estimate. Supporting extract: “multi-agent systems are not a universal solution.” [Primary research-team summary and paper link](https://research.google/blog/towards-a-science-of-scaling-agent-systems-when-and-why-agent-systems-work/).
- **Anthropic research-system engineering, June 13, 2025.** The widely repeated **90.2% improvement** is on an internal **research** evaluation, not coding. Token use is a major explanatory factor; multi-agent systems used about **15× chat tokens**, not 15× single coding-agent tokens. The post explicitly warns “most coding tasks involve fewer truly parallelizable tasks than research.” It recommends outcome evaluation, trace inspection, small initial eval sets, and human checking. [Primary report](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/multi-agent-research-system).

### Inferences

- **Inference:** Preserve requirements that supply otherwise missing domain facts, constraints, acceptance criteria, and verified commands. Treat duplicated repository summaries and mandatory ceremonial outputs as costs until they demonstrate benefits. The instruction-file evidence supports testing marginal information, not indiscriminately deleting safety or domain guidance.
- **Inference:** Tests and runtime checks offer more concrete feedback than an agent saying its plan or review is satisfactory. However, tests written from the same mistaken interpretation can all pass. Independent acceptance checks and human product review remain necessary.
- **Inference:** Optional stages are easier to justify than universal stages: small changes need little planning; ambiguous or cross-cutting changes may justify a short specification; long multi-session work may justify durable state; hard tasks may justify an independent evaluator.
- **Inference:** Separate the benefits of clean contexts, more inference budget, role-specific prompts, independent verification, and concurrency. A multi-agent result bundles these mechanisms unless experimentally separated.
- **Inference:** Re-run ablations after model/agent upgrades. A framework can remain useful while particular stages become redundant. Native agents also improve; comparing a current framework with an outdated bare-model baseline would be misleading.

### Gaps

- No primary evidence here establishes that more Gherkin scenarios, mandatory architecture documents, fixed planning-stage counts, or separate cleanup agents improve net local productivity.
- No universal optimal instruction length, context-reset frequency, agent count, or review-loop iteration count is established.
- No controlled evidence here establishes that repeated agent review converges to correctness. Correlated mistakes, superficial approval, scope growth, and evaluator gaming remain possible.
- Capability demonstrations and vendor posts do not settle long-run maintainability, escaped-defect rates, security, or total ownership cost.

## 3. What minimal local trial would fairly compare native agents with SpecFlow?

### Takeaway

Run a small randomized crossover pilot on real backlog work with the same model, native agent, repository, tools, and acceptance standard. Charge SpecFlow for creating and maintaining its specifications and compare against a competent native workflow with native planning and verification enabled, not an intentionally weak one-shot prompt.

### Cited Findings

- **Methodological basis:** METR randomized tasks only after developers submitted descriptions, recorded implementation time, and showed why self-estimates are insufficient. Its later study identifies task withholding, noncompletion, concurrent work, and quality differences as measurement hazards. [Original methods](https://metr.org/blog/2025-07-10-early-2025-ai-experienced-os-dev-study/); [follow-up methodology lessons](https://metr.org/blog/2026-02-24-uplift-update/).
- **Budget/scope basis:** Anthropic's full-stack example spent over twenty times the solo inference budget and increased scope, so that comparison cannot isolate the value of orchestration. [March 24 comparison](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/harness-design-long-running-apps).
- **Outcome basis:** The current AGENTS.md paper finds more testing and cost without statistically established task-success improvement, while writing-versus-shipping research warns against activity metrics. Evaluate accepted work, not stage completion, tokens spent, commits, or the number of generated tests. [Instruction experiment](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.11988v3); [shipping research](https://www.nber.org/papers/w35275).

### Inferences

The following is a **proposed pilot design**, not an empirically validated sample-size calculation.

1. **Freeze the question and treatment.** Primary estimand: does adding SpecFlow to the same native local agent reduce human time to an accepted change without worsening quality? Pin model identifier, reasoning setting, agent version, SpecFlow commit, permissions, available tools, machine, dependency state, and global/repository instructions. Record what is actually loaded. Scope is local work; no hosted review or issue-to-code services.
2. **Choose 12–20 real tasks before assignment.** Use consecutive eligible backlog items rather than cherry-picked AI-friendly examples. Include small fixes, tests/refactors, normal features, and several ambiguous or cross-cutting changes. Record category, expected size, acceptance criteria, developer familiarity, and risk before randomization. Twelve to twenty tasks are a practical screening pilot, not enough to declare a small effect proven.
3. **Block and randomize.** Pair roughly comparable tasks by size/type and randomize one per pair to native and one to SpecFlow. Balance treatment order over time. Use the same developer across both conditions to reduce skill confounding; allow a few unscored familiarization tasks for both first. Do not have the same human solve exactly the same task twice as the primary comparison: the second attempt benefits from the first solution.
4. **Give both arms the same initial information.** Both receive the same kind of task brief and access to existing domain/repository docs. Native may inspect code, ask questions, use plan mode, native subagents where normally available, write a useful lightweight plan, test, and iterate. SpecFlow may generate its staged artifacts, but their generation, review, corrections, and context transfers count. Do not provide SpecFlow-produced artifacts to native for free while calling this an end-to-end framework comparison; that would answer a different, downstream implementation question.
5. **Avoid existing-context leakage.** Use isolated worktrees/fresh conversations and prevent access to the competing arm's artifacts. Keep unrelated settings consistent. If a repo is already heavily SpecFlow-shaped, disclose that the trial measures the incremental workflow on a SpecFlow-prepared repo, not the total value of SpecFlow setup. Track reusable setup separately and report both first-use and amortized costs.
6. **Predefine the stopping and rescue rules.** Use the same maximum wall-clock/API-cost budget and acceptance deadline per size category, generous enough to avoid mechanically truncating one arm. Allow normal human intervention in both arms, but time and classify it. Record overruns and incomplete tasks rather than dropping them. If the framework normally uses more budget, report the resulting cost/quality tradeoff; optionally run a separate equal-inference-budget diagnostic later.
7. **Primary metric: human active minutes to accepted output.** Include task clarification, planning/spec review, prompts, monitoring, manual edits, diff review, test triage, rescue, and corrections. Separately record elapsed time, agent execution/wait time, model cost/tokens, interruptions, clarification turns, unplanned scope, and work abandoned. Distinguish true parallel productive work from merely excluding waits. Do not add concurrent-agent wall times and label the sum developer time.
8. **Apply a common independent acceptance gate.** Freeze required behavior before treatment; use existing regression tests, task-specific acceptance checks withheld from agents where feasible, build/type/lint checks, and a human diff/product review. Review security/access boundaries and unnecessary abstractions or dependencies. Agent-generated tests are deliverables, not the sole grading oracle. Blind the quality reviewer to the workflow where practical; preserve artifacts for later diagnosis. Score both initial submission and time/cost after required fixes.
9. **Check delayed cost.** Over the next one to two weeks, log regressions, rework, and defects attributable to each change. A single developer can also revisit a sample to explain or extend the code, recording difficulty. Treat this as limited follow-up rather than proof of long-term maintainability.
10. **Analyze transparently.** Publish the per-task table, completion counts, matched-pair human-time ratios/differences, median and total human time, spend, quality failures, and uncertainty intervals where supportable. Analyze failures and rescues, not just successes. Look separately at simple and complex work, but do not present tiny post-hoc subgroups as established effects. Predeclare a practical adoption threshold, for example a meaningful human-time reduction with no serious quality loss; the exact threshold is the developer's choice.
11. **Then isolate causes.** If SpecFlow helps, compare full SpecFlow with a lean variant on subsequent tasks: concise intent/acceptance criteria plus implementation and independent verification. Remove one stage at a time (extensive upfront design, mandatory tests-first ceremony, extra evaluator pass, repeated cleanup), measuring what changes. If native wins, retain only framework components tied to observed failures or documentation needs. Recheck after a meaningful model/agent upgrade.

**Suggested recording schema:** task ID; base commit; size/type/risk; assigned workflow; model/agent/framework versions; shared brief; start/end; human minutes by activity; elapsed and waiting minutes; agent cost/tokens; clarifications/interventions; acceptance checks; first-pass result; time/cost to repair; out-of-scope changes; delayed defects; final accepted/rejected status.

**Interpretation rule:** a pilot win means “useful for this developer, task mix, repo, and pinned agent configuration.” It does not prove universal superiority. A noisy result means collect more comparable tasks, not manufacture a confident conclusion. Keep any team-governance or durable-documentation benefit separate from claimed task-speed gains.

### Gaps

- A statistically powered sample size cannot be specified before observing task-time variability, within-developer correlation, failure rates, and the minimum worthwhile improvement. The pilot should supply those quantities.
- Total framework setup/maintenance and benefits over months may outweigh or reverse per-task findings. A short trial can identify obvious overhead and failure prevention, not settle lifetime ROI.
- This assignment did not inspect or modify SpecFlow implementation code, run coding experiments, or benchmark any model. The trial is a recommendation grounded in the verified research limitations, not a result.
